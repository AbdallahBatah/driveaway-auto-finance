Two car loans can show the same monthly payment and cost very different amounts. The difference is hidden in the interest rate and the length of the loan. If you learn to read three numbers (APR, term and total cost), you can compare any offer in a minute.

## What APR actually means

APR stands for annual percentage rate. It expresses the yearly cost of borrowing, including interest and certain fees, as a percentage. A lower APR means you pay less for the same loan. When comparing offers, compare APR to APR, not "interest rate" to "APR," since they are not always the same thing.

## What the term does

The term is how long you take to repay, usually stated in months. A longer term lowers the monthly payment but increases the total interest because you borrow the money for more time. A shorter term raises the payment and cuts the interest.

## See the effect in numbers

The figures below are illustrative. Assume a $14,000 loan at 14.9 percent APR.

- **36 months:** the payment is about $485 a month, with roughly $3,400 in interest.
- **48 months:** about $389 a month, with roughly $4,700 in interest.
- **60 months:** about $332 a month, with roughly $6,000 in interest.
- **72 months:** about $295 a month, with roughly $7,300 in interest.

Going from 36 to 72 months cuts the payment by about $190 but costs you roughly $3,800 more. That is why the longest term should be a last resort.

## The number that matters most: total cost

Ask for the total of payments, which is the monthly payment multiplied by the number of months, plus any fees and the down payment. Then compare it with the price of the car. The gap is what borrowing costs you. Seeing that figure makes it easier to decide whether a slightly higher payment on a shorter term is worth it.

## Other items that change the cost

- **Fees:** documentation, title and registration, which may be added to the loan.
- **Add-ons:** extended warranties, GAP coverage and similar products. Some are useful for some buyers, but each one should be optional and priced separately, and you should know what it covers.
- **Prepayment rules:** can you pay extra or pay off early without a penalty?
- **Late fees and grace periods:** know them before you sign.

## A simple method to compare offers

1. Get the APR, term, monthly payment, down payment and fees from each lender in writing.
2. Calculate total of payments plus down payment for each.
3. Adjust for vehicle price so you compare the same car.
4. Pick the lowest total cost whose monthly payment still fits your budget.

You can experiment with numbers using the [payment calculator](/financing/) before you talk to anyone. For how this plays out with dealer-provided loans, see [what in-house financing is](/blog/what-is-in-house-financing/).

## What if my rate is high?

With limited credit, a higher APR may be unavoidable at first. Reduce the pain by increasing your [down payment](/blog/down-payment-on-a-used-car/), choosing a less expensive car and picking the shortest term your budget allows. After a year or more of on-time payments, ask about refinancing if your credit improves.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a lower monthly payment always better?

No. A lower payment on a longer term often costs more in total. Judge the offer by total cost and by whether the payment is comfortable.

### Can I pay off a car loan early?

Often yes, and it saves interest. Ask whether the contract has a prepayment penalty and whether extra payments reduce principal.

### What is a good APR?

It depends on your credit, the age of the vehicle and the lender. Rather than chase a number, compare real offers on the same car and term.
