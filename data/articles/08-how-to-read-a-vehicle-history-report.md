A vehicle history report is a record of events tied to a car's VIN: titles, registrations, reported accidents, odometer readings and service entries. It is one of the cheapest ways to avoid a bad purchase, but only if you know how to read it and remember what it leaves out.

## Start with the VIN

Every car has a 17-character vehicle identification number. Check that the VIN on the report matches the one on the dashboard, the door jamb sticker and the title. If any of them differ, stop and ask why. A mismatch is a major red flag.

## The sections that matter most

### Title history

The title tells you how the state has branded the vehicle. Be careful with brands such as salvage, rebuilt, junk, flood, lemon buyback or odometer rollback. A branded title does not make a car unusable, but it usually reduces value, can make insurance and financing harder and deserves a thorough independent inspection. A clean title is a good start, not a guarantee.

### Accident and damage records

Look at the number of reports, the severity noted and whether airbags deployed. A minor cosmetic incident is different from structural damage. If something is listed, ask for repair documentation and have a mechanic check the area. Also remember that accidents handled privately and never reported will not appear.

### Odometer readings

Readings recorded over time should rise steadily. Gaps are normal, but a reading that goes down, or a sudden drop in the average miles per year, deserves a question. Compare the last recorded mileage with what the dashboard shows.

### Ownership and usage

Number of owners, length of ownership and type of use (personal, rental, fleet, commercial) all give context. Many owners in a short time can signal a problem car. Fleet and rental vehicles are not necessarily bad, but they tend to have higher mileage for their age and heavier wear.

### Service history

Regular oil changes and scheduled maintenance entries are a good sign. Few or no records are common, so use them as a plus rather than a requirement.

### Open recalls

Recalls are free to fix at a franchise dealer for that brand. Ask whether any recalls on the VIN are open and who will complete them.

## What a report cannot tell you

A report only shows what was reported to the data sources. It does not tell you the current condition of the brakes, tires, engine or suspension, and it will miss problems that never reached a record. That is why you should combine it with the [inspection checklist](/blog/used-car-inspection-checklist/), a [test drive](/blog/how-to-test-drive-a-used-car/) and, for a car you are serious about, a mechanic's inspection.

## Questions to ask based on what you see

- Can you explain this entry and show me the repair paperwork?
- Is the title clean and in the dealer's name?
- Have all open recalls been addressed?
- Are there service records, and may I see them?

More prompts are in [questions to ask a used car dealer](/blog/questions-to-ask-a-used-car-dealer/).

## Use it to compare, not just to reject

A report lets you rank similar cars. Between two otherwise equal vehicles, the one with consistent records, fewer owners and a clean title usually deserves a higher price, and the other deserves a discount or a pass. Explore similar vehicles in the [inventory](/inventory/) and compare up to three side by side. Mileage also matters, so read [how many miles is too many](/blog/how-many-miles-is-too-many/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do all dealers provide a history report?

Many do, and you can ask for one for any car you are seriously considering. If a dealer cannot or will not provide one, you can purchase a report using the VIN.

### Does a clean report mean the car is in good shape?

No. It means no negative events were reported. Condition must be checked in person.

### What is the worst thing a report can show?

Typically a branded title (flood, salvage or odometer rollback) or evidence of structural damage. Treat these as serious and get expert advice.
