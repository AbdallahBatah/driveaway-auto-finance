Winters in Lake and Porter counties mean cold mornings, lake-effect snow and a lot of road salt. A used car that handles all that without drama is not about the badge on the hood. It is about the right tires, a healthy battery, a body that is not rotting and a few habits that keep the car working all season.

## Choose a car that suits winter

### Drivetrain

- **Front-wheel drive** is a good, economical base for snow, because the engine weight sits over the driven wheels.
- **All-wheel drive** helps you accelerate on slick roads and is useful for hills and unplowed side streets, but it adds cost and complexity.
- **Four-wheel drive** in trucks and some SUVs is strong in deep snow and off-road conditions.
- **Rear-wheel drive** works fine with proper tires and careful driving, though it is the least forgiving.

Whichever you pick, tires matter more than the badge. See [SUV or sedan](/blog/used-suv-vs-sedan/) for how the body styles compare.

### Ground clearance and size

More clearance helps in deep snow, while a smaller, lighter car can be easier to maneuver. Think about your driveway, the roads you drive on and whether you park outside.

## Look for salt damage

Road salt causes rust. When you shop, check the wheel arches, rocker panels, door bottoms, the underside and the brake lines. A vehicle that spent its life in the region can look fine from above and be rough underneath. Our [inspection checklist](/blog/used-car-inspection-checklist/) walks through where to look.

## Tires are the winter upgrade that matters

All-season tires are acceptable in mild weather, but they stiffen and lose grip in the cold. Dedicated winter tires use a softer compound and tread designed for snow and ice, and they shorten stopping distance. If you drive early commutes or on unplowed roads, they are the most valuable safety upgrade you can buy. At minimum, make sure tread is healthy, tires are the same size and pressures are checked often because cold air lowers pressure.

## Battery and starting

Cold weather exposes weak batteries. If the battery is more than a few years old, have it tested before winter. Check the terminals for corrosion and look at the date code on the battery. A weak battery that works in September can fail in January.

## Fluids, wipers and visibility

- Use washer fluid rated for freezing temperatures.
- Replace worn wiper blades and keep a good ice scraper in the car.
- Check coolant strength with a tester and keep it at the proper level.
- Make sure the heater, defroster and rear defogger work. Test them on a [test drive](/blog/how-to-test-drive-a-used-car/).

## Brakes and lights

Brakes should be in good condition before the snow arrives. Check all exterior lights and replace dim bulbs. Visibility matters when daylight is short.

## A small winter kit

Keep these in the car from November on:

1. Ice scraper and snow brush.
2. A small shovel.
3. Jumper cables or a jump pack.
4. A warm blanket, gloves and hat.
5. A flashlight and phone charger.
6. A bag of sand or cat litter for traction.

## Habits that protect the car

Rinse the underside after salty weeks, when temperatures allow. Keep the fuel tank at least half full to reduce condensation and give you a buffer in a delay. Give the car a moment to warm up gently, then drive smoothly without hard acceleration.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need AWD in Northwest Indiana?

Not necessarily. Many drivers do well with front-wheel drive and good tires. AWD is useful if you live on unplowed roads or park on a hill.

### When should I put on winter tires?

A common rule is when daytime temperatures stay below about 45°F, since the softer compound works better in the cold.

### Does rust always mean a bad car?

Surface rust is common and often cosmetic. Rust through the frame, brake lines or structural areas is a serious problem and a reason to walk away.
