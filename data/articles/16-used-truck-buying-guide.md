A used pickup can be the most useful vehicle you own, or a very expensive lesson. Trucks are often used hard, so the history and condition of the individual vehicle matter more than the badge. Start with the job it has to do, then inspect carefully.

## Match the truck to the work

- **Light duty, daily driving and weekend hauling.** A midsize pickup such as a Chevrolet Colorado is easier to park, gets better fuel economy and handles most home and hobby tasks.
- **Heavier hauling and towing.** A full-size pickup such as a Chevrolet Silverado 1500 offers more payload and towing capacity.
- **Work use.** Consider bed length, cab size, payload and whether you need four-wheel drive.

Do not guess the towing capacity. It depends on engine, axle ratio, cab, bed and equipment, and the number is listed for the specific truck. Look at the door-jamb sticker and the owner's manual, and ask the dealer to confirm what that VIN is rated for.

## Look hard at how it was used

A truck that hauled landscaping stone every day lives a different life than one used for commuting. Check for:

- **Bed wear.** Deep gouges, dents and a bent tailgate indicate heavy loads.
- **Hitch wear.** A worn receiver or trailer wiring suggests regular towing.
- **Interior wear.** Heavy wear on the driver's seat and pedals compared with the mileage.
- **Aftermarket changes.** Lift kits, oversized tires and engine tuning can stress parts and complicate warranty and insurance.

## Frame and underbody

Road salt is hard on trucks. Get underneath and look at the frame rails, cross members, brake lines, fuel lines and the bottom of the cab and bed. Surface rust on steel is normal. Flaking, bubbling or holes in the frame or brake lines are serious. A lift or a safe look from a mechanic helps. Read our general [inspection checklist](/blog/used-car-inspection-checklist/) too.

## Drivetrain checks

- **Transmission.** Shifts should be smooth under light and heavy throttle, with no slipping or harsh engagement.
- **Four-wheel drive.** Test every mode on a loose surface if possible. Listen for grinding or clunks.
- **Differentials and axles.** Whining, clunking when turning or leaking seals deserve attention.
- **Engine.** A cold start with no knocking, smoke or rough idle. Check the oil for metal or a milky look.
- **Cooling system.** Towing generates heat, so make sure temperatures stay normal on the test drive.

## Test drive differently

Drive it loaded, if you can, or at least on a rough road and at highway speed. Check steering play, braking distance and how the truck behaves over bumps. An empty truck rides stiffly, so do not judge comfort by that alone. The [12-point test drive route](/blog/how-to-test-drive-a-used-car/) works for pickups too.

## Mileage and age

Trucks can run for a long time when maintained, so condition and service records matter more than a single number. A well-maintained high-mileage truck can be a better buy than a neglected low-mileage one. See [how many miles is too many](/blog/how-many-miles-is-too-many/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a diesel truck a better buy?

It depends on how you use it. Diesels can be strong for heavy towing but cost more to buy and maintain. For most light-duty use, gasoline is simpler and cheaper.

### Should I buy a four-wheel-drive truck?

If you drive on unplowed roads, tow on slippery surfaces or work off-road, yes. Otherwise, two-wheel drive with good tires is cheaper and lighter.

### How can I verify a truck's towing capacity?

Use the door-jamb label, the owner's manual and the VIN-specific information from the manufacturer or dealer. Do not rely on a general model number.
