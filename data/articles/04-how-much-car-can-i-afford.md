The honest answer to "how much car can I afford?" is that it depends on your income, your other bills and how much cash you can put down. The price on the windshield matters less than the monthly cost of owning the car. This guide gives you a method you can finish in ten minutes.

## Start with the whole cost of ownership

A car payment is only one line. Add up these monthly costs:

- **Loan payment:** principal and interest.
- **Insurance:** call your insurer with a couple of example vehicles, because rates differ a lot between models.
- **Fuel:** estimate your weekly miles and the vehicle's fuel economy.
- **Maintenance and repairs:** set aside a small amount every month, even on a newer used car.
- **Registration and taxes:** spread the yearly cost over twelve months.

## Rules of thumb that help

Financial planners often suggest keeping total transportation costs to a modest share of take-home pay. One popular guideline, sometimes called 20/4/10, suggests putting about 20 percent down, financing for no more than four years and keeping total car costs under about 10 percent of gross income. It is a guide, not a law, and many households stretch it, especially when credit is limited. Use it as a check on whether a deal is reasonable, and adjust honestly if your situation requires it.

## A worked example

Suppose you take home $3,200 a month and want transportation to stay around 15 percent of that, which is $480. If insurance is $130, fuel is $120 and you set aside $50 for upkeep, that leaves about $180 for the loan payment. That number, not the sticker price, is what tells you which cars to look at. At a typical used-car rate over a few years, $180 a month supports a much smaller loan than most people expect, which is why a down payment makes a real difference.

You can test your own numbers with the [payment calculator](/financing/). Change the down payment, the term and the rate and watch how the monthly figure moves.

## Why a longer term is a trap

Stretching a loan to 72 or 84 months lowers the monthly payment but increases the total interest and leaves you owing more than the car is worth for longer. If a payment only works at the longest possible term, the car is probably too expensive. See [how APR, term and total cost interact](/blog/understanding-apr-loan-term-and-total-cost/).

Cash down and a trade-in both reduce what you finance. ## Leave a cushion

Aim for a payment that still works if your income dips a little or a repair bill shows up. People who budget to the last dollar are the ones who fall behind. A slightly cheaper car with a payment you can pay without stress is a better purchase than the nicer car that keeps you anxious.

## Frequently asked questions

### What percentage of income should go to a car?

There is no single right answer. Many guides suggest keeping total car costs, including insurance and fuel, to roughly 10 to 15 percent of take-home pay. Lower is safer.

### Is it better to pay cash or finance?

If you can pay cash without draining your emergency savings, you avoid interest. If paying cash would leave you with no cushion, a moderate down payment and a manageable loan is often wiser.

### Should I include insurance in my budget?

Always. Insurance can differ by hundreds of dollars a year between vehicles, so get quotes before you commit to a specific car.
