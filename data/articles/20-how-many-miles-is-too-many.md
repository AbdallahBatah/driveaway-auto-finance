Buyers often ask for a number: "What is the maximum mileage I should accept?" There is no single right answer. Modern cars are built to last far longer than older ones, and the condition of a particular car tells you more than its odometer. Still, mileage is a useful starting point if you use it with the rest of the picture.

## What the odometer really tells you

Mileage measures how much the engine, transmission, suspension, brakes and interior have been used. More miles generally means more wear, which means closer-to-term replacement of parts such as tires, brakes, belts and suspension components. It also affects the price and sometimes the financing available.

## Miles per year

A common benchmark is about 12,000 miles per year. A five-year-old car with 60,000 miles is typical. A car with 30,000 miles after five years was lightly used, and one with 120,000 miles was driven a lot. Neither is automatically better or worse. A low-mileage car that sat for long periods can have its own problems, such as dried seals and old fluids.

## Highway miles versus city miles

Highway driving is easier on a vehicle than stop-and-go, short-trip driving. A car with 100,000 highway miles may be in better shape than one with 60,000 miles of short city trips. The history report can hint at how the car was used, as explained in [how to read a vehicle history report](/blog/how-to-read-a-vehicle-history-report/).

## Maintenance beats mileage

Regular oil changes, fluid services and repairs done on schedule make a big difference. Ask for service records and look at how the car feels on the [test drive](/blog/how-to-test-drive-a-used-car/). A well-maintained car at 110,000 miles can be a safer buy than a neglected one at 70,000.

## Mileage and the type of vehicle

- **Sedans and compact SUVs.** Many are designed to last well past 150,000 miles with proper care.
- **Full-size trucks and large SUVs.** Often last long too, but heavy use and towing wear parts faster. See [our used truck guide](/blog/used-truck-buying-guide/).
- **Minivans.** Heavy, family-use vehicles with many moving parts. Check sliding doors and the transmission. See [our minivan guide](/blog/used-minivan-buying-guide/).
- **Luxury and high-performance models.** Repairs can be expensive, so mileage and records matter more.

## Parts that matter at higher mileage

At higher mileage, ask what has been replaced and what is due. Typical items include timing belts or chains (depending on the engine), water pump, spark plugs, transmission fluid, suspension bushings, brakes and tires. Replacement costs should be factored into the price. Use the [inspection checklist](/blog/used-car-inspection-checklist/) to see what shows.

## How mileage affects price and financing

Lower-mileage cars cost more, and lenders may limit the term or require a larger down payment on very high-mileage vehicles because the car's value is lower. If you are financing, the shorter the remaining life of the car, the shorter the term you should choose. See [APR, term and total cost](/blog/understanding-apr-loan-term-and-total-cost/).

## A practical rule

Instead of choosing a cutoff, compare cars within your budget on three things: documented maintenance, how the car drives and the price relative to similar vehicles. A car with higher miles but strong records and a lower price can be the smarter purchase.

## Red flags

- Odometer readings that go down or do not match records.
- No maintenance history on a high-mileage car.
- Wear on pedals, seats and steering wheel that does not match the odometer.
- Warning lights that are on or have been disabled.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 100,000 miles too many?

Not by itself. Many cars run well past 150,000 miles. Look at the maintenance record, the condition and the price.

### Is low mileage always better?

Usually, but not always. Cars that sit unused can develop problems, and a low odometer should still be backed by records.

### How do I know if the odometer has been tampered with?

Compare the reading with the history report, service records and the wear on the interior. Mismatches are a reason to walk away or get expert advice.
